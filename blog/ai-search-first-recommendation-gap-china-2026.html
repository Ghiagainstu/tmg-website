The First-Recommendation Gap: Why "Mentioned" Is Not "Recommended" in China's AI Search

Nearly six in ten brands never appear in AI answers. Of those that do, half are only an alternative. A measurement guide for China's AI search.

TMG
Tuyue Media Gateway
Paid Media Team · Shanghai

On 11 November, Tencent launched Yuanbao Search (元宝搜索) as a standalone web search product at so.qq.com, with an entry point inside QQ Browser. Its pitch is unusually blunt for a Chinese search product: ads sit in a separate module labelled "related advertising" (相关广告), users can collapse the entire block, and results prioritise official and institutional sources. The product lead's framing was quoted across Chinese media: "We don't reject advertising, but users should not have to look for answers inside ads."

Three days earlier, China's Double 11 shopping festival closed its 18th edition. Headline growth was modest — low single digits, according to third-party monitoring — but the structural signal was not. Alibaba embedded its Qwen shopping assistant inside Taobao. JD pushed its AI shopping assistant to the front of the purchase flow. Douyin turned "ask Doubao before you buy" into a purchase entry point. And a survey of 1,000 respondents by the China Youth Daily social research centre found 66.7% used AI to help with shopping this year, with 27.3% describing it as deep involvement.

Put those two events together and the shift is clear: in China, the place where a brand gets discovered is moving from the search results page into a conversational answer. Which raises a question most brands cannot yet answer — when a buyer asks an AI for a recommendation in your category, do you appear, and are you the one it puts first?

1. The entry point really has moved

The demand-side evidence is no longer anecdotal. CNNIC's Generative AI Application Development Report (2026) puts China's generative AI user base above 700 million as of the first half of 2026, with 76% using AI to answer questions. On the supply side, every major platform has rebuilt the discovery path:

Taobao / TmallQwen shopping assistantAssistant embedded in the main app; AI hardware and "Token" top-ups emerged as a new category

For an international brand, the practical consequence is that the question is no longer "what do we rank for" but "what does the model say when asked about our category". Those are not the same optimisation problem, and the second one is not yet measured by most marketing teams.

2. Three numbers that look alike and are not

This is where most AI-visibility reporting goes wrong. Three metrics get quoted interchangeably:

MetricQuestion it answersDenominator
Mention rateWere we named at all?Number of prompts asked
First-recommendation rateWhen named, were we the recommendation rather than an alternative?Number of times the brand was mentioned
Citation rateDid the answer cite our page as a source?Number of answers checked

A brand can be mentioned in 30 of 100 category prompts (30% mention rate), appear first in 12 of those 30 mentions (40% first-recommendation rate), and have its own site cited in only 5 answers overall. Report those three percentages side by side without their denominators and the brand looks inconsistent. It is not — the metrics answer different questions with different commercial value.

The distinction matters because being an alternative is worth far less than being the default. In a conversational answer, a brand listed third in a sentence like "you could also consider…" occupies a very different position from the one named as the recommendation.

3. What the measurement actually shows

The most transparent public dataset on this comes from GeoBuddy, which publishes results from its own free AI-visibility checker rather than a survey. As of its 2 November 2026 cut (n = 2,703 brand checks, across ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Perplexity):

  • 59.7% of checked brands were never mentioned by any of the four engines for their own category questions.
  • The average visibility score across all brands was 14.6%.
  • Of brands that were visible, only 50.5% were the engine's primary recommendation — the rest appeared as an alternative.

Per-engine mention rates in the same dataset: Perplexity 9.1%, Gemini 8.9%, Claude 7.5%, ChatGPT 7.3%. Industry breakdown skews toward the sample's composition — web design and development agencies 78.1% never mentioned, developer tools 77.1%, design tools 76.9%, skincare and beauty 74.9% (n=167).

Two caveats belong in the same paragraph as the numbers:

  1. The sample is self-selected, skewed toward SaaS, digital products and agencies — people who chose to run a free visibility check. It is not a random sample of all brands, and the report says so.
  2. These are global engines, not Chinese ones. GeoBuddy measures ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Perplexity. Chinese buyers mostly ask Doubao, Yuanbao, Qwen, Kimi or DeepSeek. The direction of the finding may transfer; the percentages do not.

What does transfer is the structural insight: absence is the default. A brand does not become visible in AI answers by existing, publishing a brochure site, or buying media. It becomes visible by being the kind of source the model can read, verify and repeat.

4. China's platforms are writing the rules into the product

The interesting part of Yuanbao Search is not that Tencent built another search engine. It is the design choices, because they tell you what the model rewards:

  • 1,000亿-level index (the company's figure) covering web pages, WeChat Official Account articles, Channels video and academic literature.
  • 157 authoritative sources across 13 industries — health, sport, travel, automotive, public services and others.
  • Official and institutional results ranked first; brand official sites are positioned prominently and explicitly labelled as official.
  • Advertising physically separated into its own module, labelled, and collapsible in one click.
  • Every result carries its source, so the user can click through and verify.

Contrast this with the transaction-closed-loop path taken by Doubao and Qwen, where the assistant moves the user from question to product page to checkout without leaving the app. Two commercial models are emerging: trust through provenance (Yuanbao) versus conversion through convenience (Doubao, Qwen).

For a brand, both paths reward the same underlying asset: structured, verifiable facts that a model can lift and attribute. A beautifully designed product page whose specifications live inside images, PDFs or unlabelled paragraphs is, to a model, close to unreadable. A plain, well-structured page with parameters, use cases, certifications, service scope and pricing logic is quotable — and quotable content is what becomes a recommendation.

5. The trust gap cuts both ways

There is a counterweight that brand teams should not skip. In the same China Youth Daily survey, 53.7% of respondents worried that AI's price comparisons and bundling advice were unreliable and might cost them more, and 43.3% worried about being pushed obscure products or non-official stores. A Shanghai Consumer Council survey found 38.79% reported that AI prioritised higher-priced items.

Read together with Yuanbao's design choices, the implication is not "AI推荐 is infallible". It is that the first recommendation is only durable if it can be verified. A brand that wins the first slot through a fabricated comparison or an unverifiable claim is one sceptical follow-up question away from losing it — and, in China, potentially one enforcement action away from a much larger problem.

That is precisely where the grey market has appeared. Chinese media have documented vendors selling "pay to be recommended by AI" services, built on batch-generated fake reviews and fabricated expert commentary. Legal commentary in Chinese state media has characterised interference with AI recommendation results as potentially constituting false or misleading commercial promotion — the same category of risk that China's 2026 GEO enforcement actions have already tested.

6. A monthly measurement you can actually run

Most brands do not need a dashboard. They need a repeatable baseline. A workable minimum:

  1. Fix 20 real customer questions — category terms, scenario terms and comparison terms, phrased the way a buyer would ask, without your brand name in them.
  2. Ask them on the engines your buyers use. For China: Doubao, Yuanbao, Qwen, Kimi, DeepSeek. For global: add ChatGPT, Gemini, Perplexity, Claude.
  3. Record three numbers separately, with denominators: mentioned / prompts asked; first-recommended / times mentioned; cited / answers checked.
  4. Log the answer text and the cited sources, not just the rank. The sources tell you which third-party domains the model trusts in your category.
  5. Repeat monthly. Trends within one engine are meaningful; absolute scores across different tools are not comparable.
  6. Act on the gap, not the average. If you are mentioned but never first, the problem is positioning and proof, not coverage. If you are never mentioned, the problem is being absent from the sources the model reads.

7. What to fix first

In our experience working with international brands entering China, the first-recommendation gap usually traces back to four things, in this order:

  1. Unstructured facts. Parameters, specs, service scope and certifications buried in images or prose. Restructure them into explicit, quotable blocks.
  2. Thin third-party corroboration. A model cross-checks. If your facts appear only on your own site, they are weaker than the same facts repeated by industry media, vertical communities and reference sources.
  3. Category language mismatch. You describe the product in global positioning language; Chinese buyers ask in category and scenario terms. If your content never uses the words in the question, it will not be retrieved.
  4. No measurement loop. Without a monthly baseline, every content decision is a guess.

8. Key takeaways

  • Discovery in China is moving from the results page to the answer, and the answer has a first recommendation that is worth materially more than a mention.
  • Absence is the default: 59.7% of checked brands were never named by any of four engines; of those visible, only 50.5% became the primary recommendation.
  • Mention rate, first-recommendation rate and citation rate use different denominators — report them separately or you will misread your own performance.
  • China's platforms are encoding the rules into the product: provenance and verification on one path, transaction convenience on the other. Both reward structured, verifiable facts.
  • Consumers use AI but do not fully trust it (53.7% doubt the price advice). A recommendation that cannot be verified is not durable.
