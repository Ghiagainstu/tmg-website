China's Micro-Drama Market Hits ¥100B: Why Brands Now Buy Actors, Studios, and Series

On September 11, 2026, Ocean Engine and CTR released the 2026 Micro-Drama Industry Trend Report. The market crossed ¥100 billion in 2025 and is forecast to reach ¥121 billion in 2026, with more than 851 million users. But the commercially decisive finding sits elsewhere: viewers now choose what to watch not only by plot, but by actor, IP, and production studio. That single shift turns an unstable one-off content market into a predictable multi-season partnership market — the clearest signal yet that Chinese short drama is moving from content-driven to brand-driven.

1. The Report: Ocean Engine × CTR on China's Micro-Drama Industry

On September 11, 2026, Ocean Engine's Interactive Film & Drama division and CTR (CCTV Market Research) jointly released the 2026 Micro-Drama Industry Trend Report. It draws on a June 2026 survey of 3,088 short-drama viewers and 80 creators, supplemented by DataEye and QuestMobile data and CTR's own Xinghan mobile user panel.

Two findings matter more than the rest:

  1. The market crossed ¥100 billion in 2025 and is projected to reach ¥121 billion in 2026.
  2. The basis on which viewers choose what to watch has structurally changed. The show itself is no longer the only unit of choice — actors, studios, and series now compete for attention alongside plot.

The second point is what turns a content story into a brand story. Most coverage of this report will focus on market size. The more actionable signal is the change in how audiences decide.

2. A ¥100 Billion Market — Growing, but Slowing

2025's actual ¥100 billion significantly beat earlier expectations of roughly ¥60 billion. The growth rate, however, is decelerating: from a peak of 268% in 2023 to a projected 21% in 2026. The industry has moved from explosive expansion into a growth phase — which is precisely when content quality and brand trust begin to matter more than volume.

6. The Real Shift: Viewers Now Choose by Actor, IP, and Studio

This is the most commercially significant finding in the report — and the one most likely to be under-read.

Three supporting numbers make the pattern unambiguous:

  • 86.0% of users want stronger, more continuous IP series output in the coming year.
  • 84.9% have followed short-drama actors — and more than half actively chase that actor's next drama.
  • 72.6% have followed a production studio or production company.

Viewers have stopped asking only "what is this show about?" and started asking "who is in it?", "who made it?", and "when is the next one?" Those three questions are the clearest signal that the industry is moving from content-driven to brand-driven.

For brands, this is the critical unlock: "trusting the studio" is becoming a stable entry point for choosing what to watch. That translates into a predictable quality standard and a sustainable cooperation cycle — precisely what international brands have historically lacked in China's short-drama market.

Note the driver ranking: the brand-demand driver ranks last among creators. That is a gap, not a verdict — it suggests the supply side has not yet organized itself around brand budgets.

9. What This Means for International Brands

Five implications for brands running China marketing:

  1. The buying unit has changed. Buying a single hit show is a lottery ticket. Buying a relationship with a studio or an actor IP is an annuity. The audience data shows viewers increasingly return for the actor and the studio, not only the plot.
  2. Predictability is the real product. With 86% of users demanding serialized output and 72.6% following studios, a multi-season brand partnership produces compounding recognition instead of one-off impressions.
  3. The quality bar is now measurable. The report provides a concrete checklist — natural acting, crafted dialogue, no padding, clear audio, frame-level detail. Brand content that fails these will be filtered out regardless of budget.
  4. "Drama+" opens performance channels. Drama + e-commerce (78.0% interest among paying users) and drama + tourism (88.8%) sit closest to conversion, making them viable performance vehicles rather than pure brand plays.
  5. The addressable audience is broader than assumed. Male share rose to 48.9%, while the 50+ cohort (15.5%) and Tier-1 cities are both growing. A youth-female-only media plan leaves reach on the table.

11. Key Takeaways

  • China's micro-drama market reached ¥100 billion in 2025 and is forecast at ¥121 billion in 2026, but growth has slowed from 98% to a projected 21%.
  • The audience is structurally broadening: male share is up to 48.9%, with the 50-plus and Tier-1 segments both growing.
  • Emotional demand is shifting from strong emotion to deep resonance — "feeling understood" (43.3%, up) now outranks "comeback thrills" (37.4%, down).
  • The commercially decisive change: viewers now choose by actor (47.6%), IP (38.0%), and studio (30.5%) — not only by plot.
  • 86.0% want serialized IP, 84.9% follow actors, and 72.6% follow studios — the industry is moving from content-driven to brand-driven.
  • "Drama+" formats draw high interest, especially interactive choice (89.1%) and tourism (88.8%) among paying users.
  • Caveat: All figures come from the Ocean Engine × CTR report and the panels it cites (DataEye, QuestMobile, CTR Xinghan). They are the report's own survey findings, not independently verified third-party measurements. Treat the structural direction as reliable; treat the exact percentages as report-level estimates.
