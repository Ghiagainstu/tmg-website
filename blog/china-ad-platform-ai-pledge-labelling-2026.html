Eight Platforms Signed China's AI Marketing Pledge. Labelling Is Still Not a Defence.

On 2 November, eight Chinese advertising platforms signed a self-regulatory marketing convention, organised by the Beijing Municipal Market Supervision Administration: Baidu, Ocean Engine, Tencent, Kuaishou, JD, Meituan, 360 and Phoenix. The AI clause does two separate things, and that separation is the point. Labelling establishes that content is AI-generated. Truthfulness establishes that the thing described actually happened. Neither substitutes for the other, and Chinese legal commentary has now said so plainly: labelling does not exempt content from liability for false advertising arising from a fabricated experience.

1. What Was Signed

On 2 November, eight Chinese advertising platforms signed a self-regulatory convention on internet marketing, organised by the Beijing Municipal Market Supervision Administration.

SignatoryPosition in the Chinese ad market
BaiduSearch
Ocean Engine (巨量引擎)Douyin and ByteDance inventory
TencentWeChat and Tencent Ads
KuaishouShort video and livestream
JD GroupE-commerce
MeituanLocal services
360Search and browser
Phoenix (凤凰网)News and portal inventory

That list is close to the whole Chinese ad market. A brand running paid media in China buys from most of these, and for search and short video it buys from all of them.

The instrument is 自律公约 — a self-regulatory convention, not a regulation. It is drafted under the Advertising Law, the E-commerce Law, the Anti-Unfair Competition Law, the Consumer Rights Protection Law and the Internet Advertising Measures, and it covers graphics, short video and livestream.

Why a self-regulatory convention matters: it is what platform review teams actually implement. A law tells a platform what it must police; a convention tells you what its reviewers will flag when you submit creative. The second is more useful operationally.

2. The Four Principles

The convention is organised around four self-regulatory principles.

PrincipleWhat it commits the platform to
Value orientationContent standards for marketing; controls on material aimed at minors, including a prohibition on inducing minors into inappropriate spending
TruthfulnessNo false claims; identifiable advertising; no misleading use of absolute terms, small-print disclaimers or unattributed citations
Fair competitionNo false or misleading low-price marketing; avoid disorderly price competition that damages the category
Safety and complianceQualification checks on merchants, retention of business records, handling of complaints

Two of these are more specific than the headline suggests.

"Small-print misleading" (大小字误导宣传) is named explicitly. That is the practice of making a strong claim in large type and contradicting or qualifying it in tiny type. It has a name in Chinese enforcement vocabulary now, which means reviewers will look for it.

"Citations" (引证内容标注) is named explicitly. Any claim sourced to a third party — a survey, a test, a ranking, a statistic — is expected to carry its source and be verifiable.

3. The AI Clause

The convention's AI provision is short and does two separate things:

Note

In AI marketing scenarios, commercial promotion using deep synthesis technology must be prominently labelled, and using AI to generate false promotional content is strictly prohibited.

Read carefully, that is a conjunction, not a single obligation. Labelling is required. Generating false content is separately prohibited. Neither satisfies the other.

That distinction is the point of this article, and it is the part most brand compliance checklists get wrong.

4. The Distinction That Matters

A brand that labels its AI content has done the first thing. It has not done the second. These are independent obligations, and Chinese legal commentary has now stated that plainly.

ObligationWhat it asksWhat satisfies it
LabellingThe viewer knows the content is AI-generatedA prominent, legible disclosure — explicit label, and for video, a notice in the opening frame and surrounding area
TruthfulnessThe content is not a false representation of factThe experience or product described actually exists as described
The independence principleThe two do not substitute for each otherA labelled asset can still be false promotion

The lawyer's formulation, from commentary published on 1 November:

Note

"The AI labelling obligation and the content truthfulness obligation are independent. Labelling content as AI-generated does not exempt it from liability for false advertising arising from a fabricated experience."

This is the sentence a brand should put in front of its creative team. The common assumption — we disclosed it, so we are covered — is wrong.

5. What "Prominent" Actually Requires

"Prominently labelled" is doing more work in these documents than it appears to. The 2025 labelling measures set the baseline; the enforcement record from this Double 11 shows what fails it in practice.

RequirementWhat satisfies itWhat fails it
VisibilityA viewer understands the content is AI-generated without hunting for itA corner note in small type reading "AI effect for reference only" or "digital human display"
Placement in videoA notice in the opening frame and the area surrounding playbackA disclosure buried at the end, or in the description only
Attribution by elementThe viewer can tell which images are generatedAI model images arranged among a few real photographs
Not misleading by motionThe asset does not imply behaviour the product does not haveAI video that optimises garment drape, movement or accessory fit

The third row is the one brands most often defend and most often lose. Mixing a generated model image into a set of real photographs is not a defensible middle ground — it is the specific pattern regulators now describe as a trap, because the composition itself prevents the viewer from telling generated from real.

A workable internal test: show the asset to someone who has not seen the brief and ask which parts are AI. If they cannot answer immediately, the disclosure is not prominent, regardless of whether a label exists.

6. AI Restaurant Reviews as the Test Case

The scenario that produced that commentary is worth setting out, because it is already in commercial use.

MCN agencies have described AI-generated restaurant visits (AI 探店) as an emerging trend and a low-cost production method, and have said they are already testing it.

PracticeLegal exposure
An AI persona praises a restaurant's food and atmosphere without having visitedFabricated experience — false advertising; infringes the consumer's right to know
AI reproduces a real person's face or voice for the reviewPortrait and voice rights infringement, separate from the advertising issue
AI label present but illegibleFails the prominence standard — disclosure that cannot be read is not disclosure
AI used only to edit real footage of an actual visitGenerally treated as an efficiency tool, provided the experience was real and key facts are unaltered

The last row is the important defence. AI as a production method is not the problem; AI as a substitute for the experience being described is. Where a brand actually visited, actually tested, and only used AI to assemble or polish the asset, the exposure is materially lower.

The remedies are concrete: the merchant bears primary responsibility as advertiser, an unlabelled deep-synthesis asset attracts platform and cyberspace-administration action, and a consumer who can prove deception may claim return plus triple damages under Article 55 of the Consumer Rights Protection Law.

7. Supervision During, Not After

The convention specifies a three-stage review mechanism, and it maps almost exactly onto the local guidance published the day before.

StageRequirementParallel
Pre-review (事前审查)Creative and claims checked before publicationGuangzhou's "pre-review of scripts"
During monitoring (事中监测)Live content monitored as it runsSichuan-Chongqing's "dynamic monitoring" of livestreams
Post-inspection (事后巡检)Published content re-examined after the factGuangzhou's "proactive online sampling"
RecordsBusiness files and electronic materials retained at least three yearsGuangzhou's identical three-year retention

The convergence is the signal. A self-regulatory convention organised by a municipal regulator, and a set of local guidance documents issued the day before, arrived independently at the same three-stage structure and the same three-year retention period. That is not two organisations happening to agree; it is a single supervisory model being rolled out through two different instruments.

For a brand, the practical reading is that the review model is now stable enough to design processes against. Pre-review, monitor live, re-inspect afterwards, keep everything for three years.

8. One Step Further: Test Reports as Marketing

The same week, a revision draft of the Food Safety Law went out for comment, and one clause pushes the logic past advertising claims into evidence itself.

ProvisionEffect
Article 116No organisation or individual may use commissioned testing data, results or reports to engage in exaggerated promotion, advertising or other marketing activity, nor publish them without authorisation
Article 137Market regulators at county level and above may use IoT, artificial intelligence and video surveillance for online inspection, dynamic monitoring and evidence gathering
Article 104Online food platforms must conduct substantive review of merchants' licence and filing materials, not merely formal review

Article 116 is the one to watch. Read literally, it reaches beyond false claims to accurate ones: a genuine test report from a qualified lab, based on a real sample, could fall inside the prohibition if used in marketing without authorisation.

That reading has drawn objection. Legal commentators have noted that "without authorisation" (擅自) is undefined — the draft specifies neither an approval procedure nor an approving authority — and that this may conflict with the Legislative Law's requirement that legal norms be clear, specific and enforceable. Others have raised the question of who counts as "publishing".

This is a draft for comment, not law. It should be read as direction of travel, not a current obligation. But the direction is consistent with everything else that week: the regulator is moving from was the claim false to were you entitled to make it at all.

Article 137 deserves separate note because it is the mirror image of the AI-labelling story: regulators are adopting AI for supervision at the same time as they are restricting AI in advertising.

9. What This Adds to the 1 November Guidance

Readers of the previous article will recognise the setting. That piece covered three local compliance documents issued mid-festival by Sichuan-Chongqing, Guangzhou and Jinan. This convention is the next layer.

1 November articleThis article
InstrumentLocal guidance, issued by provincial and municipal regulatorsSelf-regulatory convention, organised by a municipal regulator
Binding characterAdministrative guidanceIndustry self-regulation
Core contentYou must label AI contentLabelling does not excuse false content
New vocabularyThree evasion tacticsSmall-print misleading, citation attribution, independence of obligations
RecordsThree years (Guangzhou)Three years (convention) — independent confirmation

Read together, the sequence is: the obligation to label was created in 2025, enforcement began during this Double 11, and the limits of labelling are now being stated. Each layer answers a question the previous one left open.

10. What Advertisers Should Change

PriorityActionWhy
P0Separate the labelling check from the truthfulness check in your review workflowThey are independent obligations; one combined check lets both slip
P0Audit any AI-generated review, testimonial or "visit" content for whether the experience actually happenedFabricated experience is false advertising regardless of labelling
P0Replace large-claim / small-print constructions with a single accurate claimNamed explicitly as a violation category
P0Verify every cited statistic, ranking or test carries its source and is verifiableCitation attribution is named explicitly
P1Add a during-campaign monitoring step, not just pre-approvalAll three instruments require 事中 monitoring
P1Move the three-year archive into a system you controlConvention and Guangzhou both require it; platform dashboards are not sufficient
P1Check that AI disclosures are legible at a glance, not corner notesProminence is the standard, and it is tested by readability
P2Flag any marketing use of third-party test reports for legal reviewFood Safety Law draft Article 116 may restrict even accurate reports
P2Track the platforms' published review standards as they implementing this conventionA convention lands as reviewer behaviour, which changes fastest

11. Key Takeaways

  • Eight platforms signed the 《互联网营销宣传自律公约》 on 2 November, organised by the Beijing Municipal Market Supervision Administration: Baidu, Ocean Engine, Tencent, Kuaishou, JD, Meituan, 360 and Phoenix.
  • Four self-regulatory principles: value orientation, truthfulness, fair competition, and safety and compliance. Coverage spans graphics, short video and livestream.
  • The AI clause does two separate things: deep-synthesis commercial promotion must be prominently labelled, and AI-generated false promotional content is prohibited.
  • Labelling and truthfulness are independent obligations. Labelling content as AI-generated does not exempt it from liability for false advertising arising from a fabricated experience.
  • AI restaurant reviews are the live test case. An AI persona praising a restaurant it never visited is fabricated experience; using AI only to edit footage of a real visit is generally treated as an efficiency tool.
  • The merchant, as advertiser, bears primary responsibility. Consumers who can prove deception may claim return plus triple damages under Article 55.
  • Two demands are named explicitly and often missed: small-print misleading (大小字误导宣传) and citation attribution (引证内容标注).
  • The review model is now three-stage: pre-review, during monitoring and post-inspection, with records retained at least three years — matching Guangzhou's guidance issued the day before, independently.
  • The convergence is the signal. One municipal regulator and three local guidance documents arriving at the same structure indicates a single supervisory model being deployed through different instruments.
  • A Food Safety Law revision draft goes further: Article 116 would bar using commissioned test data, results or reports in marketing without authorisation — reaching even accurate reports. It is a draft for comment, and legal commentators have objected that "without authorisation" is undefined.
  • Article 137 of the same draft authorises regulators to use AI, IoT and video surveillance for online inspection — regulators are adopting AI for supervision while restricting it in advertising.
  • This article summarises a published self-regulatory convention, press commentary and a legislative draft for information purposes and is not legal advice. A self-regulatory convention is not a statute; a draft for comment is not law. Confirm current requirements with the platforms' published standards and with counsel before changing live creative.
