China Advertising Law: The 2026 Compliance Guide for International Brands

Article 9 carries a RMB 200,000-1,000,000 fine - and one of its clauses has no objective test. A practical guide to the rules international teams actually need.

TMG
Tuyue Media Gateway
Paid Media Team · Shanghai

On 12 November 2026, the market supervision authorities of Shanghai, Jiangsu, Zhejiang and Anhui published twelve exemplar illegal-advertising cases. The first involved a short video posted by the operator of a Kuaishou "official flagship store" for a chocolate brand. The regulator described the video as packaging infidelity as a "sweet experience" and thereby "promoting incorrect views on relationships and contradicting good social morals." In October, the Putuo District authority had fined the operating company RMB 800,000 under the Advertising Law.

Two details matter more than the headline. The penalty fell on the third-party store operator, not on the brand owner — yet Article 2 of the Advertising Law defines an advertiser as anyone who designs, produces or publishes an ad "by itself or by entrusting others." And the legal basis was not a false claim. It was Article 9(7), a clause with no objective test that most global legal review workflows never examine.

This guide sets out the rules international teams actually need when they advertise in China. It is not a substitute for local counsel.

1. Why China's advertising rules catch global teams off guard

Most multinational review processes are built around substantiation: is the claim true, is the data documented, is the testimonial genuine. China's Advertising Law contains all of that, but adds a second axis — orientation and social values. Article 3 requires ads to be "expressed in a healthy form" and to conform to "socialist spiritual civilisation" and the promotion of fine traditional Chinese culture. Article 9(7) prohibits content that "hinders public order or contradicts good social morals."

There is no checklist for that second axis. It is applied by local regulators, in local cultural context, to content that is often produced by a local partner under commercial pressure. In our experience, this is the single largest gap between a global brand's compliance process and what Chinese enforcement actually examines.

A second structural difference: in China, almost any commercial content can be an advertisement. Article 2 covers direct or indirect introduction of goods or services through any medium. A store page, a seeded review, a livestream, a knowledge-sharing post with a shopping link — all can fall within scope. Many international teams classify these as "content" or "community" and route them away from advertising review entirely.

2. Who is liable: the four roles, and why outsourcing does not transfer them

Article 2 defines four roles: advertiser (广告主), advertising operator (广告经营者, design/production/agency), advertising publisher (广告发布者), and endorser (广告代言人). Article 4 states that the advertiser is responsible for the truthfulness of ad content. Article 32 requires the advertiser to commission operators and publishers with lawful qualifications; Article 30 requires written contracts between the parties.

Three consequences are frequently missed:

  • Entrusting does not exit the advertiser role. The statutory definition explicitly includes commissioning others. Legal commentary in Chinese state media takes the position that a brand normally remains the advertiser and that an internal indemnity arrangement with an agency cannot be raised against a regulator. That is commentary, not a finding in the November case.
  • Self-publishing creates record-keeping duties. Article 13 of the Measures for the Administration of Internet Advertising (2023) expressly covers ads published through a brand's own website, app, official account or online store page. The advertiser must build and update an advertising file and retain it for at least three years from the end of publication.
  • Livestreaming splits responsibility. Article 19 of the Measures assigns advertiser, operator/publisher and endorser roles across the seller, the room operator and the host, depending on what each actually does.

Article 34 and Article 14 of the Measures require operators and publishers to verify supporting documents, check content, and employ staff familiar with advertising law or establish a review function. A contract that specifies sales targets and commissions but says nothing about content approval is the pattern regulators repeatedly encounter.

3. Article 9: the eleven prohibitions

Article 9 is the highest-risk provision in the law because it carries a RMB 200,000–1,000,000 fine under Article 57. Ads must not:

#Prohibition
1Use or implicitly use the national flag, anthem or emblem; military flag, anthem or emblem
2Use or implicitly use the name or image of state organs or their staff
3Use "national-level" (国家级), "highest-level" (最高级), "best" (最佳) or similar terms
4Damage national dignity or interests, or disclose state secrets
5Disturb social stability or harm the public interest
6Endanger personal or property safety, or disclose private information
7Hinder public order or contradict good social morals
8Contain obscene, pornographic, gambling, superstitious, horrific or violent content
9Contain ethnic, racial, religious or gender discrimination
10Harm environmental, natural resource or cultural heritage protection
11Other circumstances prohibited by law or administrative regulation

Three of these are disproportionately relevant to international brands:

Item 3 — superlatives. Global brand language is full of "world-leading", "No.1", "the best" and "most advanced". These travel into Chinese landing pages, marketplace listings and social posts through translation. In the same November case batch, a Zhejiang company was fined RMB 50,000 for 69 social posts using "最全 / 最美 / 最高清" (most complete / most beautiful / highest resolution). Enforcement guidance on absolute wording exists, but the safe operational rule is simple: remove superlatives from Chinese copy unless a defensible, documented basis exists.

Items 1, 2 and 4 — state symbols and national dignity. The most common failure is visual, not textual. In the same batch, a property developer was fined RMB 60,000 because a map in its sales office omitted the Diaoyu Islands, Chiwei Yu and the South China Sea islands, and lacked a map review number. Any China-market map, flag, award or "official" visual needs a China-specific review. This is a map compliance requirement, not a translation question.

Item 7 — good social morals. This is the November chocolate case. It has no objective test, and it applies to tone and narrative, not just claims. A concept that reads as playful irony in one market can be read as endorsing behaviour that undermines social norms in another. The only reliable control is a native-language, in-market review with authority to stop publication.

4. Claims, data and superlatives

Beyond Article 9, four provisions do most of the work in day-to-day review:

  • Article 8 — representations about performance, function, origin, quality, ingredients, price, producer, validity period or promises must be "accurate, clear and unambiguous". Bundled gifts must disclose type, specification, quantity, duration and method.
  • Article 11 — data, statistics, survey results, abstracts and quotations must be true and accurate and indicate their source; where they have a scope of application or validity period, that must be stated. A global research statistic dropped into a Chinese deck without source and date is a common defect. In the November batch, a restaurant was fined RMB 10,000 for a "No.1 brand" claim backed by a third-party certificate built on a partial, single-dimension evaluation model.
  • Article 12 — patents require the patent number and type; expired, withdrawn or ungranted applications cannot be advertised.
  • Article 28 — defines false advertising, including goods or services that do not exist, material mismatches with actual product information, fabricated or unverifiable research/statistics used as proof, and fabricated results of use.

Article 33 is easy to overlook: using another person's name or image in an ad requires prior written consent (and guardian consent for minors). Repurposing user-generated content, creator footage or an executive's image across Chinese channels needs a documented permission chain.

5. What counts as an ad online

The Measures for the Administration of Internet Advertising (SAMR Order No. 72, effective 1 May 2023, replacing the 2016 interim measures) is where most China-specific operational surprises sit.

ProvisionRequirement
Art. 9Internet ads must be identifiable. Paid-ranking results must be conspicuously marked "广告" (advertisement) and distinguished from natural results. Knowledge-sharing, experience-sharing and product-review content that promotes goods or services and attaches a purchase link must also be conspicuously marked "广告".
Art. 10Pop-up and splash ads need a clear close control with one-click closing — no timer, no false or hard-to-find close button, no two-click closing, no re-pop during the same page view
Art. 11No deceptive system/error/notification prompts, fake playback controls or false reward promises to induce clicks
Art. 13Advertiser responsible for truthfulness; self-published ads need an up-to-date file retained ≥3 years; content changes must be notified to partners in a confirmable written form
Art. 14Operators/publishers must verify identity and documents, check content, retain records ≥3 years, and staff a legally competent reviewer or review body
Art. 15Where ads are published through algorithmic recommendation, the recommendation rules and ad placement records must be entered into the advertising file
Art. 18For ads containing links, the next-level landing page content related to the front-end ad must also be checked
Art. 16Platforms must monitor, investigate and stop illegal ads, retain records and provide complaint channels

Article 9 is the one that reshapes content marketing. A Xiaohongshu note, a WeChat article, a KOC review or an unboxing video with a shopping link is an ad and must be labelled. Article 14 of the Advertising Law separately requires ads in mass media to be conspicuously marked and prohibits disguised news-format advertising. Article 8 of the Measures prohibits health/wellness knowledge content from being used to disguise medical, drug, device or health-food advertising.

Two further points matter for programme governance. Article 7 requires pre-publication review for medical, drug, device, pesticide, veterinary and health-food ads (and other categories designated by law), and says reviewed content must be published exactly as approved — no clipping, splicing or editing; changes require a fresh review. And Article 15 of the Measures means an automated or algorithmic placement chain needs its rules and logs retained, not just its creative.

7. The penalty ladder

Penalties are calibrated to the type of breach, and the gap between categories is large enough to shape where review effort belongs.

BreachProvisionPenalty on the advertiser
Article 9 / 10 prohibited content (incl. superlatives and good social morals)Art. 57RMB 200,000–1,000,000; licence revocation in serious cases
False advertisingArt. 553–5× ad spend, or RMB 200,000–1,000,000 where spend cannot be computed or is clearly low; 5–10× or RMB 1,000,000–2,000,000 for three breaches in two years or other serious circumstances; possible licence revocation
Sector-specific breaches (Art. 16/17/18/23/24/25/26, unapproved review)Art. 581–3× ad spend or RMB 100,000–200,000; serious cases 3–5× or RMB 200,000–1,000,000

Operators and publishers who knew or should have known of a breach face parallel penalties, including confiscation of ad fees. Under Article 56, consumers may claim civil damages, and for health- or life-related goods, operators, publishers and endorsers can bear joint liability. Article 67 puts violations on the public credit record.

One mitigating path is worth documenting: Article 28 of the Measures allows lighter treatment or no penalty where the advertiser, operator or publisher can show it discharged its responsibilities, took measures to prevent linked content from being tampered with, and can provide the real name, address and contact details of the actual violator. That is an argument you can only make if the records exist.

8. A pre-publication checklist for China

  1. Assign the advertiser role explicitly. Name the legal entity, confirm whether publishing is direct or commissioned, and put written contracts (Art. 30) and qualification checks (Art. 32) in place.
  2. Give one in-market reviewer stop authority. Someone who reads the language and the cultural context must be able to block publication — not just flag it. This is the only control that addresses Article 9(7).
  3. Strip superlatives and absolute wording from Chinese copy, including translated global boilerplate, marketplace listings and social captions.
  4. Substantiate and cite every number — source, scope and validity period (Art. 11); verify patent numbers (Art. 12).
  5. Label native and paid content. "广告" on paid ranking, on knowledge/experience/review content with purchase links, and on any disguised news-format placement.
  6. Check the whole chain, not the creative. Front-end ad plus next-level landing page (Measures Art. 18); reviewed ads published exactly as approved (Art. 7).
  7. Secure image and endorsement permissions in writing (Art. 33, 38), and confirm the endorser has actually used the product.
  8. Keep the file. Creative versions, approval records, supporting documents, placement records and algorithmic recommendation rules, retained at least three years (Measures Art. 13–15).
  9. Prepare the response, not just the approval. In the November case, the store's customer-service reply — that the video was "personal creation" and did not represent the brand — drew as much criticism as the video. Response scripts are part of the content control surface.
  10. Re-review on every change. A modified reviewed ad needs a fresh review; a changed self-published ad must be notified to partners in a confirmable form.

9. Key takeaways

  • China's law adds a values-based test on top of substantiation; Article 9(7) has no objective checklist.
  • Entrusting production does not remove the advertiser role; official store accounts and livestreams carry the brand's name and its duties.
  • The most expensive everyday error is a superlative — a RMB 200,000–1,000,000 exposure created by a single translated word.
  • Native content with a purchase link is an ad and must be labelled.
  • Records are a defence, not just paperwork: a three-year file is what makes the mitigation in Measures Article 28 available.
